# Original video and its central claims

Richard Wilkinson, **How economic inequality harms societies**, TEDGlobal 2011 (July 2011). TED published the linked YouTube recording on 24 October 2011. The video is a public presentation of the argument behind this project, not a replacement for its underlying data and method sources.

## Central claims

The following are the project's paraphrases of Wilkinson's statements. They document his presentation and do not assert that its comparisons, mechanisms or policy conclusions have been established by this project. A descriptive comparison can be followed by an explanation or recommendation in the same passage; those are distinguished here.

| Video passage | Wilkinson's central statement, paraphrased | Type of statement |
|---|---|---|
| [00:55–02:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZ7LzE3u7Bw&t=55s) | Across the affluent countries he displays, average national income has little association with life expectancy, while within England and Wales health follows a strong social gradient. He interprets this contrast in terms of relative income and social position. | Descriptive comparisons followed by an interpretation |
| [02:18–03:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZ7LzE3u7Bw&t=138s) | He measures inequality using the incomes of the richest fifth relative to the poorest fifth and describes substantial differences between rich market democracies. | Measurement description |
| [03:06–04:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZ7LzE3u7Bw&t=186s) | The equally weighted index combines health and social indicators with social gradients. He reports that the index is strongly associated with inequality, but not average national income. | Method description and reported associations |
| [04:14–05:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZ7LzE3u7Bw&t=254s) | He offers the independently assembled UNICEF child-well-being index as another example of an association with inequality rather than average income. He then generalises that income differences matter more than further national income growth for well-being in rich societies. | Reported comparison followed by a broader interpretation |
| [05:31–06:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZ7LzE3u7Bw&t=331s) | He reports lower trust in more unequal societies and says the work was repeated separately across the 50 U.S. states, with similar relationships. | Reported associations and a claim of replication |
| [06:45–09:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZ7LzE3u7Bw&t=405s) | He presents mental illness, homicide, imprisonment, school dropout and intergenerational mobility as further examples of worse outcomes associated with inequality. He attributes much of the imprisonment difference to sentencing rather than crime rates. The homicide display includes U.S. states and Canadian provinces. | Reported associations plus a specific explanatory claim |
| [09:29–11:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZ7LzE3u7Bw&t=569s) | He describes a broad pattern across outcomes. Comparing Sweden and Japan and referring to state results, he argues that greater equality can be achieved through redistribution or smaller earnings differences before tax. | Cross-outcome generalisation and a policy-relevant interpretation |
| [11:08–12:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZ7LzE3u7Bw&t=668s) | Using infant mortality by social class in Sweden and Britain and referring to other studies, he argues that the benefits of greater equality extend beyond the poorest, while being greatest at the bottom. | Reported comparisons and a generalisation about who benefits |
| [12:44–14:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZ7LzE3u7Bw&t=764s) | He proposes that inequality intensifies status competition, insecurity and fear of social judgement. He cites laboratory stress research as support for the relevance of social-evaluative threat. | Proposed psychosocial mechanism and supporting evidence cited by the speaker |
| [14:20–15:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZ7LzE3u7Bw&t=860s) | He says available observations from the chosen sources are included, rejects selective data use, refers to a larger literature, and says other researchers have adjusted for factors such as poverty and education. | Statements about data selection and the literature |
| [15:30–16:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZ7LzE3u7Bw&t=930s) | He explicitly notes that correlation alone does not establish causation, then discusses chronic social stress and sensitivity to being looked down on as causal explanations for some outcomes. | Causal caveat followed by causal/mechanism claims |
| [16:06–16:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZ7LzE3u7Bw&t=966s) | He recommends addressing both pre-tax and post-tax inequality, constraining top incomes and making employers accountable to employees, and concludes that reducing income differences can improve quality of life. | Policy recommendations and a causal claim |

## Relation to this reanalysis

The project reproduces the comparisons selected in its [result catalogue](../config/results_catalogue.csv) under the [research method](../RESEARCH-METHOD.md). This summary does not automatically add every example in the talk to that catalogue. In particular, the laboratory stress studies, Canadian provinces, class-specific comparisons, mechanisms and policy recommendations remain contextual claims unless the agreed scope explicitly includes their underlying analyses.

The talk is evidence of what Wilkinson presented in 2011. Statements about sample construction, index calculation, source data or reported values still need to be checked against the released files and methodological documentation. The video's proposed explanations are not conclusions of the reanalysis.
